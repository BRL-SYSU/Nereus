The research

Abstract

Underwater imagery records rich ecological information and supports applications such as science communication, fishery monitoring, ecological conservation, and oceanographic research. Beyond general scene understanding, these applications require models to recognize fine-grained biological traits and reason over spatially grounded evidence. However, existing underwater datasets often provide coarse category labels, generic captions, or total-count supervision, which limits their use for organism-level analysis and ecological reasoning. We introduce Nereus, a large-scale dataset for fine-grained underwater perception that jointly supports Fine-Grained Object Attribute Understanding and Grounded Counting Perception. Nereus contains 88K images and 2.68M question-answer pairs, including annotations for 136K marine organism objects across 2,690 species and 1,515 genera, as well as 65K point annotations and 67K grounded counting QA pairs. Experiments with representative multimodal models show that Nereus improves downstream adaptation over existing underwater-domain data and provides a foundation for future research on fine-grained marine perception.

01The dataset

From seeing a scene to understanding its inhabitants.

Marine perception needs more than a category label or a single count.

Nereus connects object-level biological detail with spatially grounded evidence. It supports two complementary task families for the challenging visual conditions of the underwater world.

04Dataset construction

Built from evidence. Refined through review.

Two complementary annotation pipelines connect biological knowledge, visual evidence and structured supervision.

01

Object attributes

Section 4.1 · pp. 6–7

  1. Locate

    Object localization and contextual crops.

  2. Ground

    Taxonomic knowledge priors.

  3. Discuss

    Independent models compare answers.

  4. Arbitrate

    Uncertain cases reach a larger model.

  5. Review

    Calibrated quality scoring and expert checks.

02

Grounded counting

Section 4.2 · pp. 6–7

  1. Count

    Existing IOCfish5K point annotations.

  2. Locate

    Semantic regions and spatial grids.

  3. Characterize

    Clear, ambiguous and camouflaged states.

  4. Aggregate

    Point-derived density and local hotspots.

  5. Question

    Grounded QA with spatial evidence.

Annotation quality

Model assistance, human calibration.

Qwen3-VL-8B and InternVL3-8B discuss attribute annotations; low-confidence cases go to Qwen2.5-VL-72B. GPT-4o quality assessment is calibrated by three marine-biology master's students, with expert arbitration for low-scoring test annotations.

Reported quality: 9.26/10 on 200 sampled training images and 9.14/10 on test annotations. These are quality ratings, not percentage accuracy. Sections 3.2 and 4.1; Appendix A.1.

06Qualitative evidence & limitations

The difficult cases are part of the story.

Curated annotation examples, not a model leaderboard. Dense scenes, occlusion and language-grounding errors remain open challenges.

When language and structured evidence disagree
Released QA · sample 0001, pair 3
“The densest grid area is 'top-right' (top-right corner), with 70 fish”
Structured annotation

Top-center: 87. Top-right: 70. The released answer overlooks the actual maximum. The explorer preserves the structured values; the source QA file is not modified.

A concrete annotation-quality failure, not a measured model prediction. Numeric consistency checks and semantic review remain necessary.

What Nereus does not establish

Coverage is uneven across taxa, geography, depth and imaging devices. Automated localization and multimodal annotation can introduce noise. Image-level degradation labels do not measure local water quality or hidden biological states. Results do not show universal gains across every metric, and the manuscript reports no error bars.
